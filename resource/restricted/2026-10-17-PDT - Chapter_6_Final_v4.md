For about three years now I have been giving more or less the same talk in different rooms: Stanford, San Jose State, Texas A&M, Seoul National University, Korea University, KAIST, POSTECH. The slides change and the audiences change, but the first question from the back of the room never does. Someone stands up, usually a student but sometimes a professor who has waited politely through forty minutes of convex optimization and cancer biomarkers, and asks whether AI is going to replace us. When it is not that question, it is its twin: so AI is just a tool, right?

For a while I answered, and I had a good answer, with charts. Then I noticed that however I answered, the room left with exactly the picture it had come in with: humans on one side and machines on the other, a line between them. The only question worth asking was where that line would sit next year. My answer had not touched the picture. It had only decorated it.

The question changes its clothes with the audience but not its shape. In rooms full of policymakers rather than students, nobody asks whether AI will replace anyone; they ask how far behind the country is and how fast it will need to run to catch up. I have given numbers in answer to that, and watched them settle into the same picture: a race with a track, a finish line somewhere ahead, and the only question being our position on the track. Nobody asks who laid out the track, or what is waiting at the end of it.

This chapter is about why I stopped answering those questions, and two others like them.

Part I of this book was the story of a pattern. At Samsung I found the best possible DRAM design and never once asked who had decided what "best" meant. At Amazon I helped ship an algorithm worth two hundred and seven million dollars and could not have told you whether it had made a single person's life better. At Gauss Labs I watched people on a factory floor refuse to trust an algorithm. At Erudio Bio I found that before health data can be used to save a life, someone has to answer the prior and much harder question of who owns it. Each time the technical question got answered, and each time the real question was never asked. Part II asks why, and the honest place to start is with the questions everyone else is asking, because those are the ones that crowded out the real one.

There are four of them: human versus machine, technology as destiny, hype versus fear, and regulation as follower. Each one sounds like a question. Each one is also a frame, and a frame decides in advance what will be allowed to count as an answer.

I learned what that means from optimization before I learned it from anything else. When you set up a problem, the first thing you write down is not the objective. It is the set of things the solver is allowed to consider, and everything outside that set is invisible to it, no matter how clever the algorithm or how much computing power you give it. If the best design in the world lies outside the set you wrote down, the solver will never find it, and will report its best answer with complete confidence. A question works the same way on a mind. Ask "when will AI replace us" and you have written down a set in which every answer is a date and a direction. The things that lie outside that set are not wrong. They are simply unreachable from where you started.

That is why this chapter is about questions rather than answers, and why I will keep returning to one question in particular: when a way of asking becomes the obvious way, who benefits from its being obvious? The four frames did not become dominant by being true. They became dominant by being useful to someone, and the someone is usually not the person asking.

Four frames, then, taken one at a time. For each I will try to say three things: what it asks, what it hides, and who gains from the hiding. At the end there is a table that puts the four side by side, and by then I hope they will look less like four separate mistakes and more like one. I should also say what this chapter does not do. It dismantles. It does not yet build. The building begins in Chapter 7, and it would be dishonest to start it before the ground is clear.

## The Human vs. Machine Trap

### The date that never arrives

Start with the version of the question that comes with a date attached: when will artificial general intelligence arrive? The answers range from "already here" to "never," and a great deal of money and anxiety rides on the spread between them. I want to set the answers aside and look at the question itself, which is doing more work than any of the answers.

The forecasting record is worth a moment, because it is long and it is consistent. In 1950 Alan Turing predicted that by the end of the century a machine would hold a five-minute conversation well enough to fool an ordinary questioner about a third of the time. In 1957 Herbert Simon, who would later win a Nobel Prize in economics, predicted that within ten years a computer would be the world chess champion and would prove an important new mathematical theorem. In 1970 Marvin Minsky told Life magazine that in three to eight years we would have a machine with the general intelligence of an average human being. These were among the most capable people of their century, and each was wrong in the same direction, with the same confidence, about the same thing.

Then look at what happened when the machines did arrive. In 1997 a machine beat the world chess champion, and chess stopped being the measure of intelligence roughly the following week. In 2016 the same thing happened to Go, which had been the fallback measure. In 2020 it happened to protein folding, a problem that had defeated laboratories for fifty years and was supposed to be different because it was about nature rather than games. Nobody held a meeting to move the goalposts. They moved on their own, which is what goalposts do when they were never anchored to anything, and so the date keeps arriving and never arrives.

The question has not gone away; it has been industrialized, with surveys whose median estimate shifts by a decade between editions and reports that pick a year and build a scenario around it. What interests me is that a question answered wrongly for seventy years is still the first one asked, and the reason is not that the answer is useful. It is that the question is useful. It raises money, it frightens, and above all it defers: as long as the date is somewhere ahead, every harder question can wait until it arrives.

When a forecast fails for seventy years in the same way, the lesson is not that the forecasters are bad at their jobs. It is that the thing being forecast is not a thing. "When will AI arrive at human level" presupposes a finish line, and a finish line presupposes a single track. A single track presupposes that intelligence is one quantity, that it comes in amounts, and that human beings sit at some known mark on the scale, waiting to be passed. Every link in that chain is doing work, and none of them survives contact with the record.

### The intelligence spectrum fallacy

The fallacy underneath is easy to see once you put two systems side by side, both built on the Transformer, the architecture behind most of what now gets called AI. One reads medical images and flags pancreatic cancer months before a radiologist would catch it. The other produces disinformation at industrial scale, thousands of plausible articles an hour, each slightly different so that no filter catches them all. Which one is more intelligent? The question has no answer, and not because we lack data about either system. It has no answer because placing both on a single axis erases the only fact about them that matters, which is what each was built to do and for whom. The intelligence spectrum is a ruler laid across things that do not share a dimension.

The robotics community noticed a version of this forty years ago and gave it a name. Moravec's paradox is the observation that the things humans find hardest, such as symbolic reasoning, chess, and calculus, turned out to be easy for machines. The things any four-year-old does without effort, such as recognizing a face, walking across a cluttered room, or knowing that a cup will spill, turned out to be fiendishly hard. If intelligence were one quantity, that could not happen; a machine that was "smarter" than a child would be smarter at everything. The paradox dissolves as soon as you drop the single axis, which is to say it was never a paradox about machines. It was a paradox about the ruler.

There is a distinction I have come to rely on, and it comes from watching people rather than reading about them. There is knowing how something works, there is knowing how to make something work, and there is knowing what is worth making work and for whom. The first two can be taught, tested, and, increasingly, automated. The third is a different kind of thing, and I have met many brilliant people, and built several brilliant systems, that had a great deal of the first two and none of the third. The spectrum question quietly assumes that the third is simply more of the same, further along the same ruler. Nothing in my experience suggests that.

A footnote worth saying out loud, because almost nobody does: nobody actually uses a large language model. People use systems with a model embedded somewhere inside, wrapped in retrieval and tools and policies and an interface that someone designed. The model at the core is, in plain terms, a very good estimator of which word is likely to come next given everything that came before. That is a description, not an insult. It makes "how smart is it" the wrong unit of measure, in the way that "how strong is it" is the wrong unit for a bridge. A bridge is strong in some directions and not others, by design, for a particular river.

### The consciousness question

The trap's deepest room is the consciousness debate. Does the machine understand what it says? Is it aware? Could it suffer? These are real questions with a long literature, and I am going to set them aside in a few paragraphs, for a reason I want to explain.

Whatever understanding or awareness is, it is known from the inside, by whoever has it. Nobody has ever observed another person's experience directly; we infer it from behavior and from the fact that the other person is built the way we are. For a machine built nothing like us, the inference has no footing, so the question can only be settled from a position nobody outside the machine can occupy. But every decision we have to make about these systems is made from outside, and no regulator, hospital, or court will ever have access to the inside answer, because there is no instrument that reads it off.

Try the thought experiment the other way. Suppose it were settled tomorrow, beyond dispute, that some machine was conscious. We would still need to decide who may deploy it, for what, with what error rate, and who answers when it fails. We would have acquired a new obligation toward the machine, which is no small thing, but we would not have discharged a single one of our obligations toward each other. A question whose answer changes nothing you are about to do is a question you can set down.

So for the purposes of this book the consciousness question is not wrong so much as idle. It produces no leverage on the decisions in front of us, and a great deal of the current conversation consists of people pulling on it anyway. I am going to leave it where it lies, with respect, and get on with the questions that move something.

### Replace or assist: what actually happened to me

What I cannot leave alone is the trap's most popular form, because I fell into it myself and stayed there for years. Replace or assist? Will the machine take the job, or help with the job? Every panel I have sat on has eventually arrived at this question, and every panelist, including me, has given one of the two answers.

Let me tell you what actually happened to me. I finished my PhD at Stanford under Stephen Boyd with a command of convex optimization that fewer than a hundred people in the world share. I also left with a private embarrassment that I told almost no one about: I did not understand duality the way Boyd did. I could use it and I could teach it, but I could not see it, and that stayed true through Samsung and Amazon and a startup, for more than a decade. Then, over a stretch of weeks, I spent hours at a time in dialogue with an AI system about duality. I asked, got an answer, pushed back because the answer was so often wrong in instructive ways, corrected it, added what I knew, and asked again. Somewhere in that back-and-forth the thing I had been missing for twelve years clicked into place, and it has stayed in place.

The system did not understand duality, and I want to be exact about that. It was not a teacher, because a teacher knows the destination. It was closer to a sparring partner who never tires and never takes offense, and whose mistakes were as useful as its answers. Defending a point against a wrong objection is how you find out whether you actually hold it. What it gave me was something I had never had and could not have arranged: the experience of arguing with a hundred versions of the handful of people who could have argued back, all of them patient and none of them busy. The understanding happened in me. Nothing happened in it.

Once I had seen the shape of that, I started seeing it everywhere, and it is easiest to show with a few scenarios of the kind I now use in lectures. Picture a physician who asks the system for fifteen differential diagnoses. She dismisses twelve of them in under a minute, because she has examined the patient and the system has not, and spends her attention on the three she would not have thought of alone. Picture a biotech researcher staring at an anomalous result with seven candidate explanations. The system proposes experiments that would discriminate between them; she knows which of those experiments her lab can actually run by Friday, and that knowledge is the whole difference between a list and a plan. In each case the person supplies the thing the system lacks. The physician supplies the patient. The researcher supplies the lab.

There is an older version of this that everyone already understands. Every conference paper is online before the conference begins, and yet people still fly across oceans to sit in rooms and hear them read aloud, because the value of a conference was never the papers. It is the network effect of intelligence: the question from the floor that reframes the talk, the coffee-break conversation that becomes a collaboration. AI does not replace that network. It is the first thing in history that gives a single person access to something like it at any hour, on any subject, with no one keeping score.

Neither "replace" nor "assist" describes that. Replace is wrong on its face, since I was not replaced but changed. Assist is an understatement that misses the mechanism, because an assistant does part of your work and this did none of mine; it changed what I was capable of doing myself. The right word comes from chemistry. A catalyst lowers the barrier to a reaction and comes out the other side unchanged, with the whole transformation happening in the reactant, and so I have taken to calling what happened to me cognitive catalysis. Notice what that structure implies. In every one of the stories above, the human supplies what the system lacks: the domain, the question, the stubbornness, the desire to understand. A stronger catalyst does not make those contributions less valuable. It makes them decisive, because they become the only thing that distinguishes one outcome from another.

### Which jobs are safe? The same trap with a salary attached

The jobs question is the human-versus-machine question wearing a suit. Which jobs are safe, and which will disappear? Ask it and you can only get back lists, winners and losers sorted by occupation. The lists will be wrong in the way AGI dates are wrong and for the same reason. They assume the technology arrives with its uses already decided, so that the only thing left is to sort people into its path or out of it.

The historical record is unkind to the lists. When automated teller machines spread through American banking in the 1980s and 1990s, the obvious forecast was the end of the bank teller, and the forecast was wrong for thirty years. The number of tellers rose, because machines made branches cheaper to run, banks opened more branches, and tellers did different work in them. When the spreadsheet arrived, bookkeeping clerks declined, but accountants and analysts rose faster, doing things nobody had hired anyone to do before. In neither case did the technology decide. People decided what to do with the branches and the spreadsheets, and the decisions could have gone otherwise.

So the better question is not which existing jobs survive the machine but what new forms of work and collaboration we want to create with it. Every scenario earlier in this section implies a form of work that did not exist five years ago. There is someone who designs the catalysis, deciding what the system should be pushed on and what it should be trusted with. There is someone who verifies, since a hundred patient experts are also a hundred confident ones and somebody must know which of them to believe. And there is someone who decides the ends, which no amount of technique supplies. None of these is a job title yet. All of them are being done, informally, by people whose titles say something else, and whether they become real work is not a question the technology will answer for us. That is the paradigm question hiding under the jobs question. Chapter 8 will argue that the line between human work and machine work has always been drawn by agreement rather than discovered, and Book II returns to knowledge work in detail.

That is the skill this section is for. When you hear a question framed as humans against machines, whether it arrives as an arrival date, a ranking, a consciousness debate, or a layoff forecast, stop and name the hidden premise. It is always some version of one axis, one finish line, one contest. Then put the question back in a form we can act on. Not "when will it match us?" but "what are we building together, and who decides?"

Drop the contest, though, and something else rushes in to fill the space. If it is not us against them, then surely the technology simply advances on its own and we adjust as best we can. That reflex is the second frame.

## The Technology Determinism Myth

### "Innovation equals progress"

The word "innovation" does moral work it has never been asked to justify. It names a change and grades the change in the same breath, and you can feel the grade built into the word by noticing that nobody says "a harmful innovation" without the sentence sounding slightly off. From there it is a short step to the assumption that sits under most writing about AI, usually without being stated: innovation is progress, progress is inevitable, and the only open question is how quickly we adapt.

It was not always so. For most of its life in English the word was an accusation. An innovator was someone who tampered with settled things, and "innovation" sat in the same family as heresy and sedition. Somewhere in the twentieth century the word flipped. It kept all of its old certainty and reversed the sign, so that what had once been self-evidently suspect became self-evidently good, and the flip happened so completely that almost nobody remembers there was a flip. A word that has carried opposite moral charges within living memory is not a word that should be allowed to settle arguments on its own.

I believed the modern version for most of my career, and I had the best possible excuse. The Prologue of this book opened in a Vancouver office in August 2019, with a screen showing the result of an A/B test. Two hundred and seven million dollars in revenue, attributable to a personalization algorithm my team had built. By any definition of innovation I knew at the time, it qualified. I remember the feeling precisely, and it was not triumph. It was the feeling of having answered, brilliantly, a question nobody had asked. Progress for whom? The objective function had never been given that question, so it had no opinion, and neither, I realized standing there, did I.

### Ignoring choice and design

What the determinist frame hides is this. Every system I have ever built embedded three choices that nobody in the room called choices: what to optimize, what data to trust, and what error to tolerate. Chapter 7 takes up the first two and Chapter 11 the third, so I will only point at them here. But I want to insist that they were choices, because the frame's whole trick is to make them look like facts about the technology.

Take the proudest moment of my thirteen years at Samsung. In 2009 my group redesigned a DRAM cell with the optimization platform I had built, and the head of the DRAM team said something I still quote. It was the first time in his career, he said, that he could say with certainty a design was the best possible one. He was right, and I am still proud of it. But "best" meant best under the objective I had been handed, performance and power and area in weights that someone had set, and the physics did not choose those weights. A meeting chose them, somewhere above my pay grade and well before I arrived. By the time the problem reached me the weights had hardened into requirements that nobody thought of as anyone's decision. That, most of the time, is what technological determinism is: not a property of the technology at all, but what a choice looks like after the people who made it have left the room.

Amazon taught the same lesson at a different scale. The personalization project that produced the number on the Vancouver screen did not work the first time. The first version failed, and what changed between the failure and the success was not mainly the algorithm. It was what we decided to count, which signals we trusted and which we discarded, and where we set the bar for calling a result real. Those decisions were made by a few people in a few meetings, on the basis of what we could measure and what the company cared about. Any of them could have gone differently. The algorithm that "worked" was the algorithm that worked under those decisions. There is no version of it that worked in the abstract.

Gauss Labs showed me the same thing from the other side of the table. On a factory floor the people who distrusted our models understood perfectly well what the models did. What they understood was that the model had been given an objective that was not theirs. It optimized a quantity someone in an office cared about, and they were the ones who would be blamed when the line stopped. Their distrust was not a human-factors problem to be managed with training. It was an accurate reading of whose choice the objective had been.

And at Erudio Bio the choice is starker still, because it has a name in the statistics. A biomarker test can be tuned to miss fewer cancers at the cost of frightening more healthy people, or to frighten fewer healthy people at the cost of missing more cancers, and there is no setting of that dial that nature prefers. Someone sets it. Chapter 11 is about who, and on what authority, but I raise it here because it is the cleanest case I know of a technical parameter that is nothing but a choice about whose harm counts.

Put the three cases together and the thing determinism deletes comes into focus. You can inspect everything about a system that is made of something: the silicon, the architecture, the data, the engineers who built it. What you cannot inspect, because it is not in the system, is what the system is for. Purposes are not found in objects. They are put there by someone, and when a system is described as though it simply did what it does, the purpose has not vanished. It has been hidden, and whoever hid it is usually the one it serves.

### Historical parallels

If the technology really did determine the outcome, the same technology would produce the same outcome everywhere, and it does not. Nuclear fission works identically in every country. Yet France built most of its electricity around it, Germany shut down its last reactor in 2023, and several countries with every bit of the necessary engineering capacity never built a plant at all. More telling still is what happened after accidents. Three Mile Island, Chernobyl, and Fukushima were each the same kind of event, a reactor failing in a way its designers had said it would not. The United States stopped ordering new plants for thirty years and kept the existing ones running. Germany, whose reactors were nowhere near a tsunami, decided within months of Fukushima to leave the technology entirely. France, next door, with the same information, did not. The physics was constant and the societies were not. Whatever determined those outcomes, it was not the reactor.

The automobile tells the same story over a longer span. The car arrived in every rich country within the same two decades, and the cities those countries built around it could not be more different. The United States built highways through the centers of its cities and moved its middle class to suburbs that cannot be lived in without a car. The Netherlands began down the same road, and in the early 1970s, after a campaign against the number of children being killed in traffic, decided otherwise, and rebuilt its cities around the bicycle. Same machine, same decade, opposite cities. Anyone who tells you that a technology determines the shape of the society that adopts it has to explain Amsterdam, and I have never heard anyone do it.

Social media is the nearer parallel, because the choice is still visible if you know where to look. A feed sorted by what keeps people scrolling was not discovered inside the technology. It was chosen, in rooms I can picture because I have sat in their equivalents, and the choice was defensible on every metric those rooms were tracking. Engagement went up and revenue went up and the dashboards were green. The harms that followed, the polarization and the compulsive use and a public that could no longer agree on what had happened, arrived through exactly the mechanism that made the metrics go up. Nothing in the technology required that objective. A feed sorted by what people return to a week later rather than a minute later was always available, and whenever it was proposed it had to argue against the metric that moved faster, and it usually lost. Chapter 15 argues that we are now repeating the pattern with AI, step for step.

It is worth pausing on what "the technology made us do it" would have to mean in order to be true. It would have to mean that given the capability, only one deployment was possible: that the objective wrote itself, the data chose itself, and the error rate was fixed by nature. I have never seen a system of which any of those things was true. What I have seen, over and over, is a capability arriving in a room full of people with incentives, the incentives doing the deciding, and the decision being described afterward as what the technology did.

### Why the myth persists

There is an old move in which a person hands responsibility for a choice to an object so as not to have to own it. The driver says the car got away from him. The trader says the market moved. The engineer says the model decided. In each case something that a person did is attributed to a thing that cannot be blamed, and the person walks away lighter. Technological determinism is that move performed by an entire industry at once. The capability is treated as the agent so that the people deploying it need not be, and the treatment is so widespread that it has stopped looking like a move at all.

Which is why the myth persists: it relieves everyone. Engineers did not choose, they implemented. Executives did not choose, they responded to the market. Regulators could not have chosen, because the technology moved too fast. Investors did not choose, they followed the trend. Users did not choose, they adopted what was there. Run down the list and there is no one left in the room, and a frame in which nobody is responsible will never lack for sponsors. The question from the start of this chapter applies with full force. When a way of talking makes no one accountable, look for who is spared, because the people spared are the people who paid for the way of talking, whether or not they know it.

The myth has a geopolitical form that deserves its own sentence, because it is the one that ends arguments in the rooms where I now spend my time. If we do not build it, someone else will. The sentence is true, in the narrow sense that a capability which exists anywhere will exist everywhere eventually, and it is used to prove something it does not prove: that we therefore have no choice about what we build or how. A country that must have nuclear power can still choose its reactor design, its safety regime, and its waste policy, and the choices will matter more than the plants. The arms-race framing collapses every one of those choices into a single yes, and hands the design to whoever says yes fastest.

The skill, then, is to take any claim about what AI will do and split it in two. One part is trajectory, the genuinely new capability, and that part is real. The other part is choice, what someone decided to do with it, and that part is where the outcome lives. Then go looking for the three choice points. Who set the objective? Who picked the data? Who decided how much error was acceptable, and acceptable to whom? If you can find them, you have found the people to talk to. If you cannot find them, you are not looking at a fact about the technology. You are looking at a choice that someone has hidden, possibly even from themselves.

Determinism has two moods. In a good mood it promises abundance, cures, the end of drudgery; in a bad mood it predicts extinction. Both moods agree on the one thing that matters, which is that the outcome is coming regardless of what we do. They are one frame wearing two faces, and those two faces are the third frame.

## The Hype/Fear Binary

### The panel question

Go to any AI conference this year and wait, because it does not take long. Somewhere in the first panel a moderator will lean toward the microphone and ask the question the room has been waiting for: is AI safe, or is it dangerous? The audience sorts itself within minutes. You can watch it happen in the nods and the folded arms. And because I build these systems for a living, sooner or later someone turns to me and asks which side I am on.

The two sides are familiar enough to sketch, and each has serious people behind it. On one side is the story of abundance. AI will cure the diseases we could not cure, end the drudgery we could not escape, compress a century of scientific progress into a decade, and deliver a plenty that makes the old scarcities look quaint. On the other side is the story of catastrophe. AI will empty the labor market, flood the public square with manufactured lies, concentrate power beyond any precedent, and, in the strong version, end us. In 2023 a one-sentence statement calling the risk of extinction from AI a global priority on the order of pandemics and nuclear war was signed by the heads of the leading laboratories, among many others.

I want to be fair to both, because each is persuasive for a reason. The abundance story points at things that are real: a system that folds proteins has already changed how drugs are found, and people are alive because of detectors like the one from earlier in this chapter. The catastrophe story points at things that are equally real: the disinformation engine exists, and the concentration of capability in a handful of firms is a plain fact. Neither side is lying about its evidence. Both are wrong about what the evidence is evidence of, and the way they are wrong is a good deal more instructive than either of them.

### The category error, again

The error is one we have met already, in a different costume. AI is a capability, not an agent with intentions. A knife in a chef's hand feeds a family, and the same knife in another hand takes a life. We do not convene panels on whether knives are safe; we ask who is holding one, in what setting, and under what rules. We have built an enormous apparatus of law and custom around exactly those questions without ever once needing to settle the moral status of cutlery.

Recall the two systems from earlier in this chapter, built on the same architecture, one flagging pancreatic cancer months before a radiologist could and the other manufacturing disinformation by the thousand. If "safe" or "dangerous" were a property of the architecture, those two systems could not both exist. Since they plainly do, the property must live somewhere else, in the people and institutions that chose what to build and where to point it. The same holds, with more force, for the general-purpose systems that most people now mean by AI, because a general-purpose system is precisely one whose purpose has not yet been chosen.

Which means that "AI is dangerous" and "AI is safe" are the same sleight of hand performed in opposite directions. Both locate the moral weight in the machine, and both let the humans leave the stage. The person who says the machine is dangerous and the person who says it is safe are agreeing about the one thing that matters, which is where to look, and they are both looking in the wrong place.

### Why the evasion is tempting: three reasons, three beneficiaries

Why is that evasion so tempting? I can think of three reasons, and each one has a beneficiary.

The first is psychological. If the danger is in the machine, then the problem is technical, and technical problems are the kind we know how to make progress on. We can benchmark them, fund them, and build teams around them. The alternative is to admit that the problem is us: our institutions, our incentives, our willingness to deploy what we have not understood. Human wisdom, unlike compute, does not double every eighteen months, and there is no benchmark for it. A technical problem is simply a more comfortable thing to have than a civilizational one, and comfort is a powerful force in choosing what to believe.

The habit is old. The Luddites, who have become a byword for fearing machines, did not fear machines. The weavers who broke frames in the English Midlands two centuries ago were skilled workers who understood the new looms perfectly well. What they objected to was who owned the looms, who captured the gains, and who bore the losses. The word survives as an insult because the story was retold with the machine as the villain and the ownership question removed. We are doing the same retelling now, and calling it the safety debate.

The second reason is political, and here the two frames split the spoils between them. Consider what fear delivers. It hands governments regulatory authority they would otherwise have to argue for, and governments rarely decline authority. It hands incumbents a barrier to entry, since compliance is cheap at scale and ruinous for a startup, which is why a large company calling for strict regulation of its own industry is rarely acting against its interests. In 2023 the chief executive of the most prominent AI laboratory sat before the United States Senate and asked to be licensed. It was reported as humility, and it was also the single most effective moat anyone in the industry had proposed. Fear hands researchers a funding line. Now consider what hype delivers. It raises capital in quantities without precedent for a technology this young, it justifies concentration as the price of progress, and it defers every hard question to a future that the hyped party fully expects to control. Fear and hope disagree about everything except the one thing that matters, which is who ends up holding the pen.

The third reason is conceptual, and it is the one I find hardest to say in a room full of engineers. We have not done the slow work of building new categories. We are applying moral vocabulary built in the nineteenth century, for locomotives and bridges and medicines whose risks were bounded and local, to a technology whose reach is not bounded in that way. A bridge that fails kills the people on it. A system that shapes what a billion people see each morning has no such boundary, and our vocabulary for responsibility was never built for an object like that. Rather than build new categories we have spent a decade arguing about which of the old ones to apply, and "safe" and "dangerous" are the old ones.

Put the three reasons together and notice what the two stories have in common once you stop listening to their content. "AI safety," spoken often enough and by the right people, produces a world in which a small number of laboratories are the natural guardians of a danger that only they are equipped to measure. "AI abundance," spoken just as often, produces a world in which the same laboratories are the natural stewards of the coming plenty. The content of the two stories could not be more different. The cast is identical. When two opposed narratives install the same people in the same chairs, the opposition is not the point. The chairs are.

### The missing middle is an axis, not a midpoint

So what is in the middle? Not moderation. The middle is not "somewhat safe" or "dangerous but manageable," which are just the binary with the volume turned down, and anyone who answers the panel question with "it depends" has still accepted the question. The middle is a different axis entirely, and getting onto it takes a plain refusal to look away from the thing as it actually is.

The working rule I use is simple to state. Sort what people decide from what they do not, and spend your attention on the first list. The hype-fear binary files AI, in its entirety, under the second. It is coming; it will save or destroy; our role is to feel the appropriate emotion. Almost everything about these systems is in fact on the first list, in the plain sense that people decide it. What gets built, on what data, for whom, with what error rate, under what rules, with what recourse when it fails: every item on that list has a person's name next to it, or could. The binary's deepest damage is that it teaches people to stand in front of a thing they could shape and feel about it instead.

And the thing as it actually is turns out to be sociotechnical. No AI system is deployed into a vacuum. It arrives inside an organization with incentives, a market with competitors, a legal regime with gaps, and a population with habits, and it behaves as all of those behave. The people who study industrial accidents concluded decades ago that the worst failures in complex systems do not live in any component. They live in the interactions between components that nobody designed and nobody is watching, and that is truer of AI than of any refinery. Trying to make a system of many interacting agents safe by aligning each agent on its own is like trying to make a city safe by handing every citizen a handbook. Diagnosing the danger of an AI deployment by examining the algorithm is like diagnosing a patient by examining the left elbow. The elbow is real. It is just not where the illness is.

### Four harder questions

Once you are on that axis, the question "safe or dangerous" dissolves into four harder ones, and I want to give each of them a paragraph, because the binary survives partly by keeping them vague.

The first is institutional. What governance structures do we need for a general-purpose technology, and do they have teeth? It is easy to write a white paper; every ministry has one, and most say sensible things about transparency and oversight. What almost none of them has is enforcement capacity: inspectors who can read a system, penalties a large company would notice, and the budget to sustain any of it past the next election. A principle without enforcement is a press release. The institutional question is not what we believe but what we can make stick, and Book III is the attempt to answer it.

The second is concentration. Who gets to hold this capability, and how much of it? Consider the asymmetry. A law firm that wants a thousand more lawyers must find, hire, and pay a thousand people, which takes years and is visible to everyone. A firm with an AI legal system can deploy a thousand instances in the time it would take to interview one candidate, and nobody outside the firm need know. That asymmetry is new, and it favors whoever already has the systems, the data, and the compute. The last time a general-purpose technology concentrated economic power this fast, in the railroads and refineries of the late nineteenth century, it took a generation and a new body of law to respond. Chapter 10 and Book II take up what concentration looks like now and what, if anything, slows it.

The third is meaning, and it is the one engineers are least comfortable with. What are humans for, once most cognitive labor becomes cheap? I have written elsewhere that "why do we live" is itself a wrong question, because it assumes meaning is a thing to be discovered, lying somewhere waiting, when meaning is something created by people who decide they want it. The same move applies here, and it is the hinge of this whole section. Safety is not discovered in a system any more than meaning is discovered in a life. It is created, by people who decide they want it and build the institutions to make it. "Is AI safe?" and "Does life have meaning?" are the same kind of question, and they have the same kind of answer, which is that you are asking the wrong thing. The right question is whether we want safety enough to create it, and that question is answerable with a checkbox by no one.

There is a smaller version of the same mistake that engineers recognize at once. When a language model produces a confident falsehood we call it a hallucination and call the hallucination a bug, but the same mechanism produces the unexpected analogy and the hypothesis the researcher had not considered. A model that could not hallucinate could not surprise. Whether an output is a bug or a gift depends on what it is for and who is checking it, which is to say on the system around the model. Calling hallucination a bug mislocates the problem in exactly the way calling AI dangerous does.

The fourth is wisdom, and I will state it plainly because it is usually stated vaguely. Are we, as a civilization, mature enough to be trusted with this? The gap between what we can build and what we can govern has never been wider. Our institutions were designed for steam engines and telegraphs, for technologies whose effects arrived at the speed of a railway timetable. They are now being asked to govern a technology whose effects arrive at the speed of a software update. I do not know the answer. I know that it is a real question, that the binary prevents anyone from asking it, and that pretending we have answered it, in either direction, is the most dangerous thing in the room.

Two tests give the middle its working tools, and I use both in practice. The first: would you accept this design if you did not know which side of it you would be on? Not knowing whether you will be the patient or the radiologist, the rider or the driver, the person flagged or the person reviewing the flag. Most systems I have seen deployed would not survive that question, and most of the people who deployed them have never been asked it. Take a hiring system that screens applications. The people who build and buy it will almost always be on the reviewing side of the screen. Ask them whether they would accept its error rate not knowing whether they would be the recruiter or the applicant, and the conversation changes character at once, because the question has put them on the side of the error. The second test: which decisions should not be delegated to a system, however accurate it is? The answer is not "none," whatever the accuracy numbers say, and Chapter 8 takes up which ones.

What to do, then, when someone offers you "safe or dangerous": decline the side. Name whom each side serves, which is usually the same people. Then move to the axis where the real questions live: what institutions, whose concentration, what human purpose, and how much wisdom. You will find that the room goes quieter, and that the people who were most certain a minute ago have the least to say. That is not because they have been defeated. It is because the four questions require them to say where they stand, and the binary had been sparing them that.

And if the answer to "safe or dangerous" is design and governance, the reflex that follows is as predictable as the panel question was. Who governs? Regulators, of course, later, once they have caught up. That reflex is the fourth frame.

## The Regulation-Follows-Technology Frame

### The race, and its more serious cousin

The fourth frame comes with its own picture, and the picture is a race. Technology runs ahead on its own legs. Policy chases it, out of breath and always a few years behind, and the best anyone hopes for is that the gap does not grow. "Regulators must catch up" is how the picture gets said aloud, and it is said so often, by regulators themselves as much as by anyone, that it has stopped sounding like a claim and started sounding like a description. Notice that it is simply the second frame in a different setting. If technology moves by itself, then of course the law can only follow it. Accept determinism and the race is already lost.

The picture has a more serious cousin that I should take on directly, because thoughtful people hold it. In 1980 David Collingridge described what has since been called the Collingridge dilemma. Early in a technology's life, he observed, it is easy to shape but we do not yet know enough about its effects to know how. Late in its life we know the effects well, but by then the technology is entrenched and shaping it is expensive or impossible. The dilemma is real as far as it goes, and it is usually quoted as proof that regulation must lag. But it assumes that the only moment of influence is a deliberate act of regulation, applied to a technology that has been developing on its own in the meantime, and that assumption is the frame itself. The technology was never developing on its own. The question was never whether to influence it early or late; it was whether the influence already happening would be deliberate or accidental, visible or hidden.

### The law is already inside the technology

I want to say plainly that the race picture does not match anything I have built. No system I have ever shipped arrived in a legal vacuum and waited for the law to notice it.

At Erudio Bio the regulatory pathway was in the room from the first design meeting. What data we could touch was governed by institutional review before we touched it. What the system would be allowed to claim was shaped by how MFDS, Korea's food and drug regulator, and the FDA in the United States evaluate a diagnostic. That shaped what we chose to build, not what we did afterward. We designed the test the rules made possible, and some of the tests we did not design were not designed because of the rules. The rules were doing exactly what rules are for, before any regulator had seen a line of our code.

At Amazon the personalization system did not run wherever the algorithm pointed. Internal review processes decided what it was allowed to touch and which signals about a customer could be used, and those processes existed because of law, the threat of law, and the memory of what had happened to companies that waited for the law to arrive. The law was not behind the system. It was one of the authors of the system. And at Samsung, before I optimized a single DRAM cell, industry standards had already decided what a DRAM cell could be. Its interface, its timing, its voltages: the envelope inside which any design would have to live in order to be sold was drawn before I started. I spent years finding the best point inside a box, and the box had been drawn by a standards body years before. Who sits on standards bodies? Almost always, the engineers of the firms that can afford to send them, which means the box is drawn by the incumbents and the newcomers optimize inside it. That is law being written by people who are not called legislators, in rooms nobody calls legislatures, and the catch-up frame guarantees that nobody else is watching.

Lawrence Lessig made the phrase "code is law" famous a quarter of a century ago, and the phrase is usually read backward. By the time code is law, law was already code. Intellectual property decided what could be copied. Liability decided who would pay when something failed, and therefore what anyone was willing to ship. Corporate form decided who was accountable and to whom. Procurement rules decided which systems governments would buy, and therefore which would be built. Data access rules decided what could be learned from in the first place. Every one of these was in force before the first line of code was written, and every one of them shaped the code. "Catch up" pretends the law is absent when it is only invisible, and an invisible rule is a rule that nobody is arguing about.

That is who the frame serves. In a race where regulators are permanently behind, the leader writes the rules by default. They take the shape of defaults, terms of service, technical standards set by the firms with the most engineers in the room, and facts on the ground that are expensive to reverse. "Give us time to innovate before you regulate" sounds like a plea for freedom. It is in practice a request to hold the pen while everyone else is still finding their seats. The people who make the request know this, which is why they make it. Chapter 14 takes this frame apart in full. Here I only want to replace the picture.

### Co-design, stated

The replacement is co-design, and I can state it briefly because Chapter 14 and Book III do the real work. Error tolerance, acceptable risk, and the allocation of responsibility are decided during development whether or not anyone admits it, and earlier in this chapter we found all three hidden inside every system I have built. Co-design means deciding them on purpose, with people other than the builders in the room when the deciding happens, and with the decisions written down where they can be argued about. It does not slow the technology down. It changes who gets to say what the technology is for, and it moves that conversation from after the launch, when it is a scandal, to before the launch, when it is a design review.

Let me make it concrete with the case I know best. Imagine that the dial I described earlier, the one on a biomarker test that trades missed cancers against false alarms, were set not by the company alone. Imagine it set in a room that also held a clinician who would deliver the results, a patient advocate who had once received a false positive, and an official from the regulator who would later review the test. Nothing about that room slows the engineering. What changes is that the setting of the dial becomes a decision with authors, recorded, that each of them can be asked about later. That is all co-design is, and the regulatory sandboxes that several countries, Korea among them, have run since the late 2010s are an early and partial form of it.

### Cases where the rules came first

If that sounds like a brake, the historical record says otherwise, and I want to give several cases rather than one because the pattern matters more than any instance.

Commercial aviation did not grow despite safety regulation. In the 1920s the young airline industry in the United States went to the federal government and asked to be regulated. Passengers would not board aircraft that nobody certified, flown by pilots nobody licensed, and the industry knew it. The Air Commerce Act of 1926 was the industry's request, not its burden. Certification is what made strangers willing to board an aircraft flown by someone they had never met, and the entire passenger market was built on that willingness. The tightenings that followed the worst crashes were followed, each time, by growth in the number of people willing to fly, not by decline.

Drug approval is the same story with darker chapters. The modern American system was built in two steps, each after a catastrophe. The first came in 1938, after a liquid formulation of a sulfa drug dissolved in a poisonous solvent killed more than a hundred people, many of them children. The second came in 1962, after thalidomide. Each step was opposed as a brake on innovation, and each step built the trust on which the modern pharmaceutical market stands. A patient cannot evaluate a molecule. Review by a body the patient has reason to trust is what makes a market for medicine possible at all. Erudio Bio's path through MFDS and the FDA is not an obstacle to having customers; it is the condition of having them.

The internet, the case everyone cites for permissionless innovation, grew on shared protocols set by open bodies before anyone had scaled anything. The rules about how machines would talk to each other came first, were argued over in public, and were agreed before there was a market to protect.

Korea has its own version of this story, and it shaped the industry I later joined. In the mid-1990s the government chose a single digital standard for mobile telephony, over the objections of firms that would have preferred to wait and see, and required that it be used nationwide. The standard was unproven at commercial scale and the choice was criticized as the state picking winners. What it produced was the world's first commercial deployment of that technology and an industry that did not exist before the rule. The companies that would go on to dominate handsets for a generation grew up inside it. The rule did not follow the market. The rule made the market. Rules first, scale second, in each case.

### What law is for

Read the race picture against those cases and something comes loose. Law, in every one of them, was not a brake bolted on after the machine was running. It was a statement, made in advance, of what a community wanted from the machine, by the people responsible for the community. What a society builds reveals what it values, and law is where it says so out loud, before it builds. The catch-up frame asks us to say it afterward, in the past tense, as regret.

The fourth skill follows directly. When you hear "regulators must catch up," find the law that is already inside the system: the property rules, the liability rules, the standards, the procurement, the data access. Then ask who benefits from pretending it is not there. Usually it is whoever is currently ahead, and usually they are the ones saying it.

## Four Frames, One Structure

I promised at the start that by the end the four frames would look less like four mistakes and more like one, so here they are side by side.

**Table 6.1. The four frames of existing AI discourse**

| The frame | What it asks | What it hides | Who benefits from the hiding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human vs. machine | When will AI match us? | What collaboration are we designing, and who decides? | Whoever profits from a contest, and from selling the finish line |
| Technology as destiny | What will AI do to us? | What are we choosing, and who chose? | Everyone who would rather not be responsible |
| Hype vs. fear | Is AI safe or dangerous? | Safe for whom, decided by whom? | Incumbents, regulators, and funders on both sides |
| Regulation as follower | How do regulators catch up? | Who is already writing the rules by default? | Whoever is currently ahead |

Read down the third column and the structure appears. Each frame takes the decisive choice and moves it somewhere no one can be held to account for it: into the machine, into history, into the future, into a race that nobody can win. Four frames making the same move is not four accidents. It is what a discourse looks like when it has been shaped, over years and without anyone needing to conspire, by the people it spares. Nobody designed the four frames. They were selected, the way a river selects the stones that stay, by the simple fact that questions which threaten no one get asked more often than questions which do.

Read down the fourth column and something else appears, which is that the beneficiaries are not a cabal. They include governments and the companies governments regulate, the researchers who warn and the investors who hype. What they share is not an interest in any outcome but an interest in the choice staying hidden, because every one of them is better positioned to make it quietly than to defend it in the open. That is why the frames are so durable. They are held up from every side at once.

For readers who build these systems, which includes most of the people I have given this talk to, the four skills have a practical form, and it fits on a single page of a design review. Next to the objective: who chose it, and who was not in the room. Next to the data: who it describes and who it leaves out. Next to the error rate: who set it and who bears it. Next to the launch plan: which rules are already inside the system, and whether anyone outside the company has seen them. None of those questions is technical, and in my experience the engineers who ask them are not slower than the ones who do not. They are the ones whose systems are still running five years later.

I should say again what this chapter has not done. It has not offered a replacement. It has shown, four times, that the obvious question hides a choice and that the hiding has beneficiaries, and it has given you four habits for noticing when it happens. That is the necessary order. Frames do not fall to arguments. They fall when enough stops fitting inside them and someone offers a different frame in which the misfits turn out to be the normal case. This chapter was the pile of misfits. The chapters that follow are the offer, beginning in Chapter 7 with the claim that AI is not a technology to be controlled but a device that redistributes power and redesigns the choices available to everyone it touches. Chapter 9, the center of the book, asks why we reach for the wrong questions first, every time, and what a right question looks like.

> **Governance Insight** — *Jeff Lee and Hayden Song*
>
> The questions we ask determine the solutions we see. If we ask "When will AI be as smart as humans?" we design Turing tests. If we ask "How should AI reshape power?" we design governance structures. Current AI discourse asks the wrong questions because existing power structures benefit from those questions.

Back to the lecture hall, then, and to the briefing room. These days, when someone at the back stands up and asks whether AI will replace us, or how far behind we are, I answer with a question of my own: replace us at what, and chosen by whom? Behind whom, on a track laid out by whom, toward what? The room usually goes quiet for a moment. It is not an uncomfortable silence, or not only. It is the sound of a frame coming loose, and that silence is where the rest of this book lives.
