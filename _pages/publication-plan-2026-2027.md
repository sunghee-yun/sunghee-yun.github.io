## The FAccT 2027 fast-track — honest verdict

Twenty-two days to the abstract, twenty-nine to the full paper. The abstract is a cheap forcing function — submitting one without a paper costs nothing. The paper is the real question. My verdict: **submit the abstract on Oct 27, and decide by Nov 1 whether the draft is genuinely strong; if not, walk away silently and take the journal ladder.** FAccT is double-blind, so a withdrawn attempt burns no reputation, but a rushed weak submission wastes the one advantage you have — a thesis that is actually original. Do not attempt the sprint without a coauthor signed up *this week* (see [the collaboration campaign plan](#collaboration-outreach--the-campaign-plan)) and writing time blocked daily. If the sprint doesn't happen, nothing is lost: Ethics and Information Technology and Big Data & Society are rolling, prestigious, and arguably the better "easier first" route you asked for.

## What each paper needs before submission

Be blunt: blog essays are not papers. Every candidate needs the same four upgrades:

1. **Literature engagement.** P1 must cite the fairness/STS/governance literature it currently gestures at; P3 must engage Shanahan ("Talking about Large Language Models," 2022; clarifications, 2024) and the reversal-curse literature; P4's epistemology thread must engage philosophy of mathematical practice (Mancosu, Avigad); P5 must cite the basic-income canon (Van Parijs) to show where "existential infrastructure" extends it.
2. **A stated contribution.** One sentence per paper: what does the reader know after reading that they didn't know before? (P1: the three-mechanism account of why the safety framing persists. P2: the catalysis concept + case study. Etc.)
3. **Steelmaned counterarguments.** P1 must answer the "but alignment failures are real technical problems" objection head-on; P3 must answer the emergent-capabilities objection it already raises — and answer it harder.
4. **A coauthor where the credential matters.** Philosophy journals trust philosophers; governance venues trust governance scholars. Your engineering depth is the differentiator, not the credential — pair it.

## Collaboration outreach — the campaign plan

> You're right: collaborators first, submissions second. A coauthor changes what the paper can claim, which venue it can aim at, and how reviewers read it. And because every journal target here is rolling, waiting a few weeks for the right coauthor costs nothing — the only deadline that forces parallel work is the FAccT abstract (Oct 27), which is a free option anyway. So the sequence is: **prospectus → email → coauthor → submission**, with FAccT as the single allowed overlap.

### Your value proposition — the "why work with me" card

Write this once, then tailor one line per target. What you offer that almost no academic coauthor can:

- **Mathematical depth.** Stanford PhD under Stephen Boyd; convex optimization at the definition/theorem/proof level. You ground philosophical claims in real mathematics instead of gesturing at it.
- **Twenty years of deployment.** Samsung's iOpt platform, Amazon personalization ($200M+ revenue impact), Gauss Labs, now Erudio Bio. You've shipped the systems academics theorize about.
- **A live position at the frontier.** Biotech AI operator in Silicon Valley, right now — deployment realities, incentive structures, and failure modes seen from inside the build rooms, plus access to real systems, data, and case studies.
- **The differentiator in one line:** rigorous theory + industrial deployment + cross-domain exposition. Nobody else on their email list has all three.

Tailor the emphasis: for **philosophers** (Bender, Shanahan, Mancosu, Clark), you're technical ballast — the practitioner who builds what they theorize about. For **economists** (Nagurney, Mirowski, Danaher, Kang, Van Parijs), you're mathematical rigor plus industry reality plus a non-Western vantage. For **governance scholars** (Narayanan, Ko, Vallor), you're the operator's view — the sociotechnical thesis argued by someone inside the sociotechnical system.

### The outreach package — what you actually send

Never lead with the blog post. It's background reading, linked once at the end of the email. What you send instead:

**1. A one-page prospectus per paper (P1–P5; P6/P7 are solo).** Seven elements, one page:
1. Working title (states the thesis, not the topic)
2. The thesis in two sentences
3. The contribution — what the reader knows afterward that they didn't before
4. Target venue + deadline (shows you've done homework)
5. What you bring (from the card above, tailored)
6. What they bring (their specific work, cited — this is the flattery that isn't flattery)
7. Proposed division of labor + timeline (concrete: "I'd draft §§1–3 by [date]; you'd own the [their expertise] sections")

**2. A thesis-led email, 150–200 words.** Anatomy: subject line states the thesis ("The AI safety framing is a category error — proposal for a joint paper"); first sentence cites 1–2 of *their* specific works (proving you've read them); the thesis in two sentences; one sentence on what's in it for them; one concrete low-cost ask ("Would you be open to a 20-minute call next week? Prospectus attached."). Professors triage by the first three lines — never write longer.

### Rules of engagement

1. **One paper, one active lead.** Don't pitch P1 to Narayanan and Ko in the same week without disclosure — parallel silent pitches inside overlapping communities get found out.
2. **No email without the prospectus.** The prospectus is the proof you're serious; the email is just the envelope.
3. **One follow-up, 10–14 days, then move on.** Silence is an answer; the rolling journals don't punish you for it.
4. **Every email ends with a concrete low-cost ask** — a 20-minute call, a read of the one-pager. Never "let me know what you think."
5. **Track it.** A simple table — who, date sent, what was attached, response, next step. You'll be running twelve threads; memory won't cut it.
